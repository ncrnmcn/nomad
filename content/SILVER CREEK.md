Redwood Falls, Silver Creek County, Vermont

Overview
Redwood Falls is an unincorporated community in Silver Creek County, located in the northern wooded region of Vermont. The settlement lies near a small waterfall along Silver Creek, surrounded by dense forest, low mountain ridges, and scattered farmland. It was established in 1847 during the mid-19th-century expansion into northern New England.

History
Redwood Falls was founded following reports by early settler and surveyor Elias Hardwick, who believed reflective mineral veins along Silver Creek indicated the presence of silver. Initial excitement drew a small influx of settlers and speculators. Subsequent testing revealed the material to be muscovite-rich quartz, visually convincing but lacking economic value.

By the time the misidentification was confirmed, the settlement had already been named and informally organized. The name Redwood Falls derives from a nearby stand of large old-growth conifers and the small waterfall adjacent to the original settlement site. Limited prospecting continued into the early 1850s before being abandoned.

Following the collapse of mineral hopes, the local economy shifted toward timber harvesting, subsistence farming, and maple syrup production, industries better suited to the surrounding terrain.

Geography
Redwood Falls is situated in a narrow valley characterized by mixed hardwood and conifer forest. Silver Creek runs through the area year-round, swelling seasonally and feeding a modest waterfall that exposes mineral-streaked rock faces. After rainfall, the rocks along the falls often display a silvery sheen, reinforcing the town’s early legend.

Demographics and Culture
The population of Redwood Falls has historically remained small, never exceeding several hundred residents. The community developed as a quiet rural hamlet, with social life centered around seasonal labor, forestry, and agricultural cycles. No formal municipal government exists, and services are typically shared with neighboring towns.

Legacy
Redwood Falls is referenced in regional histories as an example of frontier optimism shaped by geological misinterpretation. While no silver was ever produced, the settlement endured, defined more by landscape and persistence than by its original promise.
